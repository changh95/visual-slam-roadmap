Basic Linear Algebra

Linear algebra is the working language of SLAM: points, poses, residuals, and observations are all vectors and matrices, and every solver ultimately reduces to matrix computations.

Vectors and Matrices

A vector v∈Rn\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^nv∈Rn is a column of nnn real numbers. In SLAM, vectors represent points, translations, velocities, and residuals. A matrix A∈Rm×nA \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}A∈Rm×n represents a linear map from Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn to Rm\mathbb{R}^mRm. The central object in SLAM geometry is the matrix-vector product Ax=bA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}Ax=b.

Determinant

The determinant det⁡(A)\det(A)det(A) of a square matrix measures the signed volume scaling factor of the linear map AAA. Key facts:

  • det⁡(A)≠0⇔A\det(A) \neq 0 \Leftrightarrow Adet(A)=0⇔A is invertible.
  • For a rotation matrix R∈SO(3)R \in SO(3)R∈SO(3): det⁡(R)=+1\det(R) = +1det(R)=+1.
  • det⁡(AB)=det⁡(A)det⁡(B)\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)det(AB)=det(A)det(B).

Dot Product and Cross Product

The dot product of u,v∈R3\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3u,v∈R3 is u⋅v=uTv=∥u∥∥v∥cos⁡θ\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\thetau⋅v=uTv=∥u∥∥v∥cosθ; it is used for projections and checking orthogonality. The cross product u×v\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}u×v produces a vector perpendicular to both, with magnitude ∥u∥∥v∥sin⁡θ\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta∥u∥∥v∥sinθ. It appears in the skew-symmetric matrix formulation of the essential matrix and in computing surface normals.

The cross product can be written as a matrix-vector product using the skew-symmetric matrix:

u×v=[u]×v,[u]×=[0−u3u2u30−u1−u2u10]\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = [\mathbf{u}]_\times \mathbf{v}, \qquad [\mathbf{u}]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -u_3 & u_2 \\ u_3 & 0 & -u_1 \\ -u_2 & u_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}u×v=[u]×​v,[u]×​=​0u3​−u2​​−u3​0u1​​u2​−u1​0​​

This little identity is everywhere in SLAM: the essential matrix is E=[t]×RE = [\mathbf{t}]_\times RE=[t]×​R, and Lie algebra elements of so(3)\mathfrak{so}(3)so(3) are skew-symmetric matrices.

Rank, Inverse, and Transpose

The rank of AAA is the dimension of its column space. In SLAM, the fundamental matrix has rank 2 by construction, and a point cloud matrix is rank-deficient when all points are coplanar (a degenerate configuration for SLAM initialization). The inverse A−1A^{-1}A−1 satisfies AA−1=IAA^{-1} = IAA−1=I and exists iff det⁡(A)≠0\det(A) \neq 0det(A)=0. For rotation matrices, R−1=RTR^{-1} = R^TR−1=RT (orthogonality) — exploited constantly to avoid computing a full inverse.

A quick NumPy check that a matrix is a valid rotation:

import numpy as np

theta = np.pi / 4
R = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta), 0],
              [np.sin(theta),  np.cos(theta), 0],
              [0,              0,             1]])

print(np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)))  # True: orthogonal
print(np.linalg.det(R))                 # 1.0: proper rotation

Solving linear systems

SLAM back-ends never invert matrices explicitly; they factorize and solve:

  • Cholesky factorization (A=LLTA = LL^TA=LLT) for symmetric positive-definite systems — the normal equations JTJ Δx=−JTeJ^TJ\,\Delta\mathbf{x} = -J^T\mathbf{e}JTJΔx=−JTe of bundle adjustment are solved this way (sparse Cholesky in practice).
  • QR factorization is the numerically safer alternative for least squares, working on JJJ directly without forming JTJJ^TJJTJ (which squares the condition number).
  • SVD is the most robust (and most expensive) option, and the only one that cleanly handles rank-deficient, homogeneous systems Ax=0A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}Ax=0.

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

SVD is the most important matrix factorization in SLAM. Any matrix A∈Rm×nA \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}A∈Rm×n decomposes as

A=UΣVTA = U \Sigma V^TA=UΣVT

where UUU and VVV are orthogonal and Σ\SigmaΣ is diagonal with non-negative singular values σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥0\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0σ1​≥σ2​≥⋯≥0. Geometrically, any linear map is a rotation/reflection, followed by a coordinate-wise scaling, followed by another rotation/reflection. Uses in SLAM:

  • Essential matrix decomposition: SVD of EEE yields the four candidate relative poses [R∣±t][R|\pm\mathbf{t}][R∣±t].
  • DLT triangulation: the solution is the right singular vector of AAA with the smallest singular value.
  • ICP alignment: SVD of the cross-covariance matrix H=∑ipiqiTH = \sum_i \mathbf{p}_i \mathbf{q}_i^TH=∑i​pi​qiT​ gives the optimal rotation.

The ratio σ1/σn\sigma_1/\sigma_nσ1​/σn​ is the condition number: it measures how much a linear solve amplifies input noise. Ill-conditioned systems (huge condition numbers) are why coordinate normalization matters in the 8-point algorithm and DLT.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

A non-zero vector v\mathbf{v}v and scalar λ\lambdaλ satisfying Av=λvA\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}Av=λv are an eigenvector and eigenvalue of AAA. The Harris corner detector classifies image patches from the eigenvalues of the structure tensor, and PCA of a point cloud uses eigenvectors of the covariance matrix to find principal directions (e.g., fitting a plane). For symmetric matrices (covariances, structure tensors, JTJJ^TJJTJ) the eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors orthogonal — and eigendecomposition coincides with SVD.

Common pitfalls

  • Explicitly inverting matrices (inv(A) @ b) instead of solving (np.linalg.solve, Cholesky): slower and less accurate.
  • Forgetting normalization before solving homogeneous systems — DLT and the 8-point algorithm degrade badly on raw pixel coordinates.
  • Sign/reflection ambiguity in SVD-based rotation recovery: always check det⁡(R)=+1\det(R) = +1det(R)=+1 and correct with a sign flip if you got a reflection.

Why it matters for SLAM

Every stage of a SLAM pipeline is linear algebra in disguise: projecting points uses matrix products, minimal solvers (8-point, DLT, ICP) reduce to SVD, and the normal equations of bundle adjustment are a large sparse linear system. Fluency here — especially with SVD, orthogonal matrices, and rank — is what lets you read SLAM papers and debug geometry code.
