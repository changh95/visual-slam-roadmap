Levenberg-Marquardt

Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) is the workhorse solver for nonlinear least-squares problems in SLAM. It is a damped version of Gauss-Newton that interpolates between Gauss-Newton (fast near the minimum) and gradient descent (safe far from it), making it much more robust to poor initialization.

From Gauss-Newton to LM

For a cost F(x)=12∥e(x)∥2F(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x})\|^2F(x)=21​∥e(x)∥2, Gauss-Newton linearizes the residual e(xk+Δx)≈e(xk)+JkΔx\mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}_k + \Delta\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}_k) + J_k \Delta\mathbf{x}e(xk​+Δx)≈e(xk​)+Jk​Δx and solves the normal equations

(JkTJk) Δx=−JkTe(xk)(J_k^T J_k)\,\Delta\mathbf{x} = -J_k^T \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}_k)(JkT​Jk​)Δx=−JkT​e(xk​)

This can diverge when the linearization is a bad approximation, or when JkTJkJ_k^T J_kJkT​Jk​ is (nearly) singular. LM adds a damping term λI\lambda IλI:

(JkTJk+λI) Δx=−JkTe(xk)(J_k^T J_k + \lambda I)\,\Delta\mathbf{x} = -J_k^T \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{x}_k)(JkT​Jk​+λI)Δx=−JkT​e(xk​)

where:

  • JkJ_kJk​ is the Jacobian of the residual vector at the current estimate xk\mathbf{x}_kxk​,
  • λ>0\lambda > 0λ>0 is the damping parameter,
  • Δx\Delta\mathbf{x}Δx is the update, applied as xk+1=xk+Δx\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \Delta\mathbf{x}xk+1​=xk​+Δx (or via the exponential map for poses on a manifold).

The two limits explain the behavior:

  • λ→0\lambda \to 0λ→0: the equation reduces to Gauss-Newton — large, aggressive steps with near-quadratic convergence close to the minimum.
  • λ→∞\lambda \to \inftyλ→∞: the equation approaches λ Δx=−JkTe\lambda\,\Delta\mathbf{x} = -J_k^T\mathbf{e}λΔx=−JkT​e, i.e. a short step along the negative gradient — slow but reliable descent.

Marquardt’s refinement scales the damping by the curvature of each coordinate, replacing λI\lambda IλI with λ diag(JkTJk)\lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(J_k^T J_k)λdiag(JkT​Jk​), so that weakly constrained directions get damped more and the method becomes invariant to per-parameter rescaling.

Adapting the damping

λ\lambdaλ is adjusted every iteration based on whether the step actually helped:

  1. Solve for Δx\Delta\mathbf{x}Δx with the current λ\lambdaλ.
  2. Evaluate the true cost at xk+Δx\mathbf{x}_k + \Delta\mathbf{x}xk​+Δx.
  3. If the cost decreased: accept the step and decrease λ\lambdaλ (trust the linearization more).
  4. If the cost increased: reject the step and increase λ\lambdaλ (take a smaller, more gradient-like step), then re-solve.

A common refinement compares the actual cost reduction against the reduction predicted by the linearized model (the gain ratio): a ratio near 1 means the local quadratic model is trustworthy and λ\lambdaλ can shrink aggressively; a small or negative ratio means the model is poor and λ\lambdaλ must grow. This is exactly the logic of a trust-region method — LM can be read as implicitly maintaining a region around xk\mathbf{x}_kxk​ within which the linearization is trusted, with λ\lambdaλ inversely related to the region’s radius.

Practical notes for SLAM problems

  • The damped system matrix JTJ+λIJ^T J + \lambda IJTJ+λI is always positive definite for λ>0\lambda > 0λ>0, so Cholesky factorization always succeeds — important for gauge-underconstrained problems such as monocular bundle adjustment.
  • The sparsity of the SLAM Hessian is preserved: damping only touches the diagonal, so the Schur complement trick for bundle adjustment works unchanged.
  • Robust kernels (M-estimators) fold into LM as iteratively reweighted least squares — weights modify JJJ and e\mathbf{e}e, the LM loop is otherwise identical.
  • LM is the default optimizer in Ceres Solver, g2o, and GTSAM, and hence the algorithm actually running inside most SLAM back-ends.

Why it matters for SLAM

Nearly every optimization in a modern SLAM pipeline — bundle adjustment, pose graph optimization, PnP refinement, camera calibration — is solved with LM. SLAM problems are highly nonlinear (projection, rotation manifolds) and initializations are often mediocre (motion-model predictions, noisy triangulations), so the pure Gauss-Newton step frequently overshoots. LM’s automatic damping is what makes these solvers converge reliably frame after frame without hand-tuning, which is why it has been the default in SLAM back-ends for decades.
